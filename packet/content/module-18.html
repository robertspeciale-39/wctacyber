Module 18 changes the question from "how does this work" to "what should I build." The exam gives you a requirement, sometimes with a diagram that has a weakness in it, and asks you to pick the design or name the flaw. The habit to build: find the single point of failure first.

Hierarchical design

Large networks are built in three layers, each with one job. Keeping the jobs separate makes the network easier to grow, secure, and troubleshoot.

LayerJobTypical devices
AccessConnects end devices: PCs, phones, printers, access points. Where users plug in and where port security and VLANs applyAccess switches, wireless APs
DistributionAggregates the access switches. Enforces policy, routes between VLANs, filters trafficLayer 3 switches, routers
CoreThe high-speed backbone. Moves traffic between distribution blocks as fast as possible and does little elseHigh-end Layer 3 switches

A small network often collapses the core, combining core and distribution in one set of devices. That is normal and is called a two-tier design. A school the size of yours likely looks like that.

The four design goals

GoalMeaningExample
ScalabilityCan grow without a redesignAdding a new building by adding an access block, not rebuilding the core
Availability / reliabilityStays up. Usually quoted as uptimeFive nines (99.999%) is about 5 minutes of downtime a year
RedundancyDuplicate devices and paths so one failure does not matterTwo uplinks from each access switch to two distribution switches
Fault toleranceThe ability to keep working through a failureWhen one link dies, traffic uses the other with no user impact

Single point of failure

A single point of failure is any one device or link whose loss takes down everything behind it. Practice reading a diagram this way: cover up one device at a time and ask "does anything stop working?" If yes, you found one.

Weak:                       Better:
 PC - SW1 - R1 - Internet    PC - SW1 --+-- R1 -- Internet
                                   \    |    /
                                    +-- R2 --+      second router, second path

Redundancy has side effects. Redundant switch links create loops, and Layer 2 loops melt a network, because frames circle forever. Spanning Tree Protocol (STP) prevents this by logically blocking the redundant links until they are needed. You get the backup without the loop.

Matching a design to a requirement

Read the requirement for its keywords, then match.

Requirement saysThink
"must keep working if one link fails"Redundant links, STP, or a routing protocol with a second path
"will add 200 users next year"Scalability: modular design, spare ports, address space planned with room
"separate student and staff traffic"VLANs and subnets, filtered at distribution
"low cost, small office"Collapsed core, one switch per area, a single router
"voice calls must stay clear"Quality of service

Quality of service

When a link is busy, something has to wait. QoS decides what goes first. The idea at this level is classification: mark the traffic that cannot tolerate delay (voice, video) and give it priority over traffic that can (email, file backup). Without QoS, a large download can make a phone call stutter.

The traits QoS protects are bandwidth (capacity), latency (delay), jitter (variation in delay), and packet loss. Voice cares most about latency and jitter. A file transfer cares about none of them as long as it finishes.

Convergence

A converged network carries voice, video, and data over one infrastructure instead of three. It saves cost and is the reason QoS exists. It also means one outage affects all three, which raises the stakes for redundancy.

Before you move on

  • Name the three layers of the hierarchy and give one job for each.
  • Given a diagram, find every single point of failure.
  • Say why redundancy needs STP, and what goes wrong without it.
  • Pick a design for each of five stated requirements and defend it in a sentence.
