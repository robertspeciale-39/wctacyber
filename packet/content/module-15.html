Module 15 is part concept, part memorization, and it is very quiz-friendly. You need to know the difference between TCP and UDP and why each exists, and you need the port numbers in your head without looking them up. The numbers are free points for anyone who drills them.

What the transport layer does

IP delivers a packet to the right device. The transport layer, Layer 4, delivers it to the right program on that device. It also decides how careful to be about delivery. It has two protocols to choose from, one careful and one fast.

The rule for choosing: if losing a piece breaks the result, such as a file or a web page, use TCP. If a late piece is worse than a missing piece, such as a live call, use UDP. A dropped fraction of a second of audio is better than a pause while it is resent.

Ports and sockets

A port is a 16-bit number that identifies a program or service. There are 65,536 of them. A socket is an IP address plus a port, for example 192.168.10.5:443, and it names one end of a conversation exactly.

When your browser opens a page, the destination port is 443 (the server's well-known port) and the source port is a random ephemeral port. That random source port is how many tabs, all talking to the same server, keep their answers separate.

The three-way handshake

TCP opens a conversation before sending data. It takes three messages.

Client                          Server
  | ---- SYN ------------------> |   "I want to talk. My sequence starts at X."
  | <--- SYN, ACK -------------- |   "OK. Mine starts at Y. I got your X."
  | ---- ACK ------------------> |   "Got your Y. Connection open."

Order: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. Closing a connection uses FIN and ACK messages. Once the connection is open, the receiver acknowledges what has arrived by sending back an ACK naming the next sequence number it expects. If an expected ACK does not come, the sender retransmits.

Flow control and windowing

A fast sender can swamp a slow receiver. TCP's answer is windowing: the receiver advertises how much data it can accept before it must acknowledge, and the sender never sends more than that. A bigger window means higher throughput; a window that shrinks tells the sender to slow down.

Why this matters for security

A SYN flood sends huge numbers of SYNs and never completes the handshake, leaving the server holding half-open connections until it runs out of room. Port numbers also matter to firewalls: a rule that blocks port 23 stops Telnet, and an unexpected open port on a device is the first thing an attacker (or a competition scoring engine) looks for.
