This module is the Packet Tracer device bar with names attached. Every icon you will drag onto a workspace for the rest of the season shows up here, and the lab half of the Practice Round is mostly "can you pick the right one and cable it correctly."

End devices and intermediary devices

An end device is where data starts or stops: a PC, a laptop, a phone, a printer, a server, an IP camera. If you can point at it and say "the message is for that," it is an end device. Every end device on a network is the source or the destination of something.

An intermediary device moves data between end devices. It is never the final destination of the user's traffic, it is the road. Switches, routers, access points, modems, and firewalls all live here.

That split is the one the quiz cares about, so learn it as a split rather than as two lists to memorize.

The four you must never confuse

Switch

Connects devices within one network. It works with MAC addresses at Layer 2, it has many ports, and it is what every wired device in a room plugs into. A switch cannot get you to a different network. Remember that sentence, because half of the device-selection questions in this course are testing it.

Router

Connects different networks to each other. It works with IP addresses at Layer 3 and it makes a forwarding decision for every packet. It is the boundary of your network and the exit toward everything else. Routers have few ports, because each port faces an entire network rather than a single device.

Access point

Extends a wired network to wireless clients. An AP is essentially a wireless switch port. It does not route, and on its own it does not hand out addresses.

Modem

Translates between the signal your ISP delivers and Ethernet. Cable, DSL, and fiber all need one. In a home, the modem, router, switch, and AP are usually one physical box, which is exactly why people get the four confused. Module 4 pulls that box apart.

A NIC, the network interface card, is the part of an end device that actually attaches to the media. It is where the MAC address lives.

Logical and physical topology

Packet Tracer gives you two workspaces, and they map onto two ways of describing a network.

The physical topology is where things sit and what cable runs where: this switch is in the closet, that cable goes through the ceiling to room 214. The logical topology is how data flows and how addressing is organized, regardless of where the boxes physically are.

Two devices can be in the same room and on different networks. Two devices can be in different buildings and on the same network. That is the whole point of separating the two views, and it is why the logical workspace is where you will spend nearly all your time.

Reading a diagram

Every topology drawing in this course follows the same conventions. Learn to read one in about five seconds:

  • A straight solid line is a wired Ethernet link.
  • A jagged or lightning-style line is a serial WAN link.
  • Dotted or wave lines are wireless.
  • A cloud is a network somebody else owns and you do not configure.
  • Labels near a line are interface names. G0/0 is GigabitEthernet 0/0, F0/1 is FastEthernet 0/1, S0/0/0 is a serial interface.

Interface naming looks fussy until the first time you configure the wrong port and spend ten minutes wondering why the link is down. The number after the slash is the port on the card. The number before it is which card.

Why this matters in the competition

The Practice Round lab hands you a written requirement and asks you to build it. You will be scored on device choice and on cabling before anything else works at all. Two habits fix most of the points lost here:

  1. Read the whole requirement before you drag anything. Count the networks it describes. More than one network means you need a router, no matter how the diagram is drawn.
  2. Check the link lights before you open a CLI. A dark link is a physical problem, and no amount of configuration will fix it.

Before you move on

  • Name five intermediary devices and say what each one connects.
  • Explain why a switch cannot reach a different network.
  • Point at G0/1 on a router in Packet Tracer without hunting for it.
