Module 8 is where the course introduces the address you will spend the rest of the season reading. Everything in Units 3 through 7 assumes you can look at an IP address and a mask and instantly know which network the host belongs to. Start building that reflex now.

What an IPv4 address is

An IPv4 address is 32 bits long. Humans do not read 32 ones and zeros, so we cut it into four groups of 8 bits, called octets, convert each octet to decimal, and separate them with dots. That format is dotted decimal.

11000000.10101000.00001010.00000101    binary, 4 octets
   192  .   168  .    10  .     5       dotted decimal

Each octet runs from 0 to 255, because 8 bits can hold 256 values. An "address" like 192.168.10.300 is invalid, and spotting that is a classic free point.

Two parts: network and host

Every IPv4 address is really two pieces glued together. The network portion says which network the device is on. The host portion says which device it is on that network. It works like a street address: the network is the street, the host is the house number.

The address alone does not tell you where the split falls. The subnet mask does. Wherever the mask has a 1, that bit belongs to the network portion. Wherever it has a 0, that bit belongs to the host portion. A mask is always a solid run of ones followed by a solid run of zeros, never mixed.

Address  192.168.10.5   11000000.10101000.00001010.00000101
Mask     255.255.255.0  11111111.11111111.11111111.00000000
                        |------ network ------|  |- host -|

Here the network is 192.168.10.0 and this host is number 5 on it.

Mask notation: dotted decimal and CIDR

The same mask can be written two ways. Dotted decimal is 255.255.255.0. CIDR (or prefix length) just counts the ones: /24. You must be able to flip between them without thinking, because exam questions switch freely.

Logical and physical addresses

A device has two addresses and they do different jobs.

MAC addressIP address
Layer2, Data Link3, Network
Also calledPhysical addressLogical address
Size48 bits, hex32 bits, dotted decimal (IPv4)
Assigned byManufacturer, burned into the NICAn admin or DHCP, and it can change
Has network structure?No, it is flatYes, network portion plus host portion
Used forDelivery on the local linkDelivery across networks

Why both? The MAC address gets a frame to the next device on the same link. The IP address gets a packet to its final destination, however many links away. Because IP addresses have network structure, a router can make a decision from the network portion alone without knowing every host in the world. MAC addresses cannot do that.

Seeing it on a real device

C:\> ipconfig

   IPv4 Address. . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.10.5
   Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.255.0
   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.10.1

C:\> ipconfig /all        adds the MAC address and DHCP details

In Packet Tracer, click a PC, open the Desktop tab, then IP Configuration, and you will see the same three values.
