Module 6 is Layer 1: the actual physical stuff that carries the signal. The questions are almost always scenarios. You get a situation and pick the media that fits it, so learn this module by its trade-offs rather than by its numbers.

The three families

Copper

Electrical signals on twisted pairs of wire. Cheap, easy to terminate, everywhere. UTP (unshielded twisted pair) is the standard office and classroom cable with an RJ-45 connector. STP (shielded twisted pair) adds foil or braid around the pairs to fight interference, and costs more.

The number to remember for copper is 100 meters. That is the maximum run for Ethernet over twisted pair. Past that the signal degrades too far to be reliable. If a scenario mentions a distance over 100 m, copper is not the answer.

Twisting the pairs is not decoration. It cancels out interference between the wires, which is why the twist rate matters and why you should not untwist more than half an inch when you terminate a cable.

Fiber optic

Pulses of light in glass. Immune to electromagnetic interference because light does not care about magnetic fields. Enormous bandwidth. Runs far: hundreds of meters for multimode, many kilometers for single-mode. It costs more, and terminating it takes skill and equipment.

Fiber is the answer whenever a scenario mentions long distance, high interference, or connecting buildings.

Wireless

Radio. No cable to run, mobile clients, and easy to add devices. In exchange you get shared bandwidth, interference, security exposure that wired media does not have, and range that walls eat.

Wireless is the answer when the scenario emphasizes mobility or the impossibility of running cable.

The three ways copper goes wrong

These three words show up constantly, and students mix them up. Learn them as three different causes.

  • Attenuation is the signal getting weaker over distance. It is why the 100 m limit exists. The cause is length.
  • EMI and RFI are outside electrical or radio noise getting into the cable: motors, fluorescent lights, power lines. The cause is the environment.
  • Crosstalk is the signal from one pair in the cable bleeding into another pair. The cause is the cable itself, usually poor termination or untwisted pairs.

Fiber is immune to EMI, RFI, and crosstalk, because it carries light rather than electricity. It still has attenuation, just far less of it.

Bandwidth and throughput, again

Module 1 introduced this and Module 6 is where it gets concrete. Bandwidth is the rated capacity of the media. Throughput is what you measure. Goodput is throughput minus protocol overhead, which is the part that is actually your file.

A Cat 6 cable rated for 1 Gbps will not deliver 1 Gbps of file transfer. Headers, acknowledgments, and retransmissions all consume some of it. That is normal, not a fault.

Straight-through and crossover

Historically, connecting two different kinds of device (PC to switch, switch to router) used a straight-through cable, and connecting two of the same kind (switch to switch, PC to PC) used a crossover.

Modern equipment negotiates this automatically with Auto-MDIX, so it rarely matters in practice. It still matters in Packet Tracer, where picking the wrong cable gives you a dark link and no explanation. When a link will not come up and the ports are correct, the cable type is the next thing to check.

Before you move on

  • State the copper distance limit without thinking about it.
  • Give the cause of attenuation, EMI, and crosstalk, one cause each.
  • Read three scenarios and pick the media for each in under ten seconds.
