Module 7 is where the course stops describing networks and starts explaining how one actually works. The switch decision you learn here is the archetypal conceptual-application question in this competition: you get a MAC address table and an incoming frame, and you predict what the switch does.

The Ethernet frame

Every frame on a wired LAN has the same shape. You do not need to memorize byte counts, but you do need to know what is in it and in what order.

FieldWhat it does
PreambleA pattern that lets the receiver sync its clock. It is not counted as part of the frame's data.
Destination MACWho this frame is for. First, because a switch wants to make its decision as early as possible.
Source MACWho sent it. This is the field the switch learns from.
Type / LengthWhat is inside: usually IPv4, IPv6, or ARP.
PayloadThe packet from Layer 3.
FCSFrame Check Sequence. Error detection over the whole frame. Fails the check, the frame is dropped silently.

Destination comes before source. That ordering is deliberate and it gets asked.

The MAC address

A MAC address is 48 bits, written as 12 hexadecimal digits, burned into the NIC at the factory. It looks like 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E or 001A.2B3C.4D5E depending on who is writing it.

The first half is the OUI, the Organizationally Unique Identifier, which IEEE assigns to the manufacturer. The second half is the serial number that vendor assigned. Together they are meant to be globally unique.

The one special value to know: FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF is the broadcast address. Every device on the local network processes a frame sent there.

MAC addresses are flat. There is no structure that says where a device is, which is exactly why they only work inside one network and why IP addresses have to exist. Module 8 picks that up.

How the switch learns

A switch boots with an empty MAC address table and fills it by watching traffic. The rule is one sentence: learn from the source, decide on the destination.

When a frame arrives, the switch records the source MAC and the port it came in on. Then it looks up the destination MAC and does one of three things.

Forward is the normal case and the whole point of a switch. The frame goes out one port instead of all of them, which is what makes a switch better than a hub.

Flood happens on a broadcast, and on an unknown unicast: a real destination the switch has simply not heard from yet. The switch is not guessing, it is covering every possibility. When the destination replies, the switch learns its port and never floods for it again.

Filter is the case students forget. If the destination is on the same port the frame arrived on, the two devices are already on the same segment and can hear each other without help. Forwarding it back out would be pointless, so the switch drops it.

Aging

Entries do not live forever. The default aging timer is 300 seconds. If a switch hears nothing from a MAC for five minutes, it removes the entry, and the next frame for that address gets flooded until the device speaks again.

That is a feature. Devices move between ports, and a table that never forgot would send frames to the wrong place forever.

Collision domains and broadcast domains

Two phrases with a clean answer:

  • Every switch port is its own collision domain. Modern switched full duplex links do not have collisions at all.
  • A switch is one broadcast domain. All of it, every port, unless you split it with VLANs, which are outside this course's scope.
  • A router boundary ends a broadcast domain. Routers do not forward broadcasts.

So: switches divide collision domains, routers divide broadcast domains.

Verifying it yourself

Switch# show mac address-table

Vlan    Mac Address       Type      Ports
----    -----------       ----      -----
   1    000a.f2aa.1111    DYNAMIC   Fa0/1
   1    000a.f2bb.2222    DYNAMIC   Fa0/2

Run this after you ping between two PCs and you will see the table populate. Run it before you ping, and it will be empty, which is the fastest way to prove to yourself that the switch learns from traffic rather than from configuration.

Before you move on

  • Say the learning rule in one sentence.
  • Given a table and a frame, produce forward, flood, or filter, and say why.
  • Explain why an unknown unicast gets flooded and why that is not a security hole in a normal switched network.
  • State the aging timer and why it exists.
