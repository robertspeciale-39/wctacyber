A network exists for one reason: to move data between devices that are not the same device. Everything else in this course is detail hanging off that sentence.

Module 1 is short and it is mostly vocabulary. Do not skip it. The Practice Round quiz comes from Modules 1 and 2, and the words here get reused in every module after this one. If "host" and "client" are fuzzy in your head now, they will still be fuzzy in Round 2 when a question depends on the difference.

Hosts, clients, and servers

A host is any device with an IP address that sends or receives data on a network. Your laptop is a host. A printer with a network jack is a host. A router interface is a host in the loose sense that it has an address, though the course usually reserves the word for end devices.

Client and server are the two roles a host can play. The client asks. The server answers. That is the whole distinction, and it is a role rather than a piece of hardware. The single most common quiz trap in this module is treating "server" as a kind of computer. It is not. Your laptop is a client when you load a webpage and a server the moment you share a folder with someone on the same network.

When every device on a small network can act as both, you have a peer-to-peer network. That is what most home networks actually are. It is cheap and simple to set up, and it falls apart at scale because there is no central place to manage accounts, backups, or security.

The size words

A LAN is a network in one place under one owner: a classroom, a house, an office floor. A WAN connects LANs across distance, and you almost never own the wire in between. You rent that connection from a provider.

The Internet is the largest example of that pattern. It is not a thing you can point at. It is an agreement between an enormous number of independently owned networks to carry each other's traffic using the same protocols. Your ISP is the company that connects your LAN to it.

Bandwidth is capacity, not speed

Students mix these up constantly, and so do adults. Bandwidth is how much data a link can carry per second, measured in bits per second. It is the width of the pipe. Throughput is how much you actually got. Throughput is always lower than bandwidth, because of overhead, interference, congestion, and the slowest link along the path.

The practical version: a 1 Gbps switch port does not make your download faster if the building's internet connection is 100 Mbps. A chain is as fast as its narrowest link.

Why this matters in the competition

Module 1 questions are pure recall, which means they are free points if you have the vocabulary and lost points if you do not. Expect the quiz to hand you a scenario and ask you to name the role, or to hand you a definition and ask for the term.

Read the question twice on vocabulary items. "Which device is acting as a server in this scenario?" and "Which device is a server?" are different questions, and the first one is the one they usually ask.

Before you move on

  • Say out loud what a network is for, in one sentence, without using the word "network."
  • Give an example of one device acting as a client and a server in the same hour.
  • Explain to someone why bandwidth and throughput are not the same number.
